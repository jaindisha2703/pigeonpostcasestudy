What is Kulfi?

If you've never opened it: Kulfi is a couples-only app made in India — two phones paired by a code, no singles, permanent dark mode. It's built for couples spending time together while apart, and most of that happens late at night.

how the app is built

Two kinds of play

Both Online games can be played any number of times a day — as long as you're both there. Turn-Based games are the daily set: roughly 12–14 of them, each played once a day. That's the part that runs out fast — 15–20 minutes and you've been through it.

and above the games

Pillow Talk

A questions space: you answer for your partner, they answer for you, and answers only unlock once you've both replied. Three are served daily, with a back-catalogue you can pull more from. When you're done with games and still missing them, you come here — and answer a few more.

S Situation — the trigger

I hit the wall as a user first

I didn't start this as a case study. I started as a Kulfi user.

The games were playful and low-pressure — a real reason to open the app, and a nice way to spend time with someone without it being another call or another conversation. But the turn-based set is finite. You play through the day's games, you answer the Pillow Talk questions, and then you hit the same quiet moment:

"I've done everything the app has. I still miss him. Now what?"

That's the moment I started designing for — not "out of games," but out of everything the app offers, and still wanting to reach him. That's when I stopped just using Kulfi and started looking at it, as a product designer.

The turn-based home — the daily set, mostly played through by night.

♥
T Task — the reframe

Finding the real problem to solve

The problem wasn't really a lack of games. Kulfi already had a growing library and added new ones every week. But the daily turn-based set exhausts fast, and once it's gone — along with the Pillow Talk questions — there isn't much left to do. (Honestly: that's an observation from my own repeated use — a hunch, not a proven user problem.)

The obvious answer was to add more games. But that only ever buys a few minutes, and it doesn't touch the actual feeling in that moment.

so the design question became
How might Kulfi keep couples reaching for each other once the day's games and questions are done — without simply adding more to play?

What if the next thing wasn't a game to play together, but something one person could make for the other? That reframe fit Kulfi more than it first seemed. Closeness across distance leans less on being online at the same time, and more on small, asynchronous gestures — rhythm over volume, something to look forward to. It echoed Kulfi's own thinking, too: retention has been their hardest problem, and the founders call games "the wedge, not the destination."

A Action — from idea to prototype

Designing Pigeon Post

The build moved through a kill, a shift, a four-part experience, and a set of details that carry the feeling.

the first direction

A cute idea with a weak mechanic

My first instinct was very physical: handwritten letters — the kind you keep and open later, tagged to a moment.

Open when you miss me Open when you've had a bad day

For a long-distance context, it felt emotionally right. But once I thought it through as a product, the interaction fell apart. How would Kulfi know the moment had come? Would anyone remember a letter was waiting? They'd just get buried.

The idea was cute. The mechanic wasn't. So I dropped it — and that turned out to be one of the more useful decisions in the project.

dropped ✕

The killed direction — "The Loft" of open-when letters.

the shift

One clear thing instead

So I simplified. Instead of messages tagged to hypothetical emotional moments, the interaction became one clear thing: when you're done playing, you can make something for your partner. Not another game. Not another question. A small gift.

And I kept it deliberately secondary. It only appears once you've exhausted the core experience — so the feature never announces "Kulfi is a gifting app now." It says something quieter: you're out of things to play, here's another way to reach them.

the experience

Create → Personalize → Send → Receive

A concept study's deliverable is the experience itself — so here it is as four beats, each a real screen from the prototype.

1

Create

You build a gift from things that feel personal — a letter, a song, a voice note, a doodle, a small memory. Each is its own object in the box, something they actually open.

2

Personalize

Then you choose how it looks — the wrapping colour, the flowers, the way the bouquet comes together. The point was to make the gift feel chosen, not generated.

3

Send

You decide the moment — now, a chosen day, or a surprise. Then a bird flies the gift across the night sky before it lands.

4

Receive

Your partner opens it, sees what's inside, and keeps it — landing in your normal chat, so it doesn't vanish with the session.

designing for delight

The small details carry the feeling

the bouquet

I started with single flowers. Feedback from someone I showed it to pushed it further — being able to compose a bouquet felt more personal. "Pick a flower" became "choose what you'd give them."

the wrapping

The sender picks the colour, based on what their partner would like — not a generic Kulfi gift, but one that looks like it was meant for a specific person.

the bird

It isn't needed for the feature to work. It's there so receiving feels like an event — because anticipation, not just the reward, is where the delight comes from.

None of it is decoration. Each detail reinforces the same thing: two people apart, and the wish to close the gap.

making it

Built in a day, through a lot of iteration

I used Claude and ChatGPT as rapid prototyping partners — rough interaction ideas to working screens fast, and visual directions I couldn't have produced by hand at that speed. But none of it was one-shot. The real work was deciding what to keep, what to cut, and what to redo.

Deciding where not to spend effort mattered as much as anything I built.

cut An early AI direction — beautiful, but far too dense
cut Open-when letters — didn't survive the interaction test
parked Flap-open animation wouldn't behave — spent the budget elsewhere
kept The pigeon flight — the moment worth protecting
R Result — what I'd validate next

An honest place to stop

This is still a hypothesis. I haven't tested whether gifting actually improves retention — and I don't want to pretend otherwise.

If I took it further, I wouldn't just ask "does it work." I'd want to understand how people actually live with it:

?

Do couples actually make gifts for each other — and how often?

?

Are they intentional about it, or is it a throwaway tap? How much time do they spend making one?

?

Does it spark a conversation between them, or just sit there once opened?

?

Which pieces get used most — voice, music, letters, doodles, memories?

?

Does it feel like a natural part of Kulfi, or a separate product bolted on?

?

Does it pull them back on the nights the games and questions are already done?

Most of all, I'd test the assumption it rests on: does giving couples something to create for each other actually answer the "nothing left to do, still missing you" moment? That's what this project can't answer yet — and for a one-day exploration, that feels like the honest place to stop.

lessons learned

What the sprint actually taught me

the real one — chasing clarity in chaos

You can't let AI decide everything for you.

Going full-AI was chaotic. You get handed an overwhelming number of directions at once, and the early prototypes come out nothing like what's in your head — the content is off, the visuals are off, everything is off. It's easy to drown in the options.

What worked was treating AI as a thinking partner to explore with — never the decision-maker. That part stays mine. The discipline was patience: stop trying to fix the whole thing at once, take one problem, settle it, move to the next. Clarity wasn't handed to me; I had to keep pulling it out of the noise, one decision at a time.

Retention isn't always more of the same

Sometimes the stronger move isn't giving people more to do, but another reason to come back.

Delight has to serve the product

The bird, the flowers, the wrapping aren't random charm — they reinforce the emotional reality of distance. If they didn't, they wouldn't earn their place.

I came in as a user who hit a wall, and left with a working answer to it — one I'd still want to keep testing.
